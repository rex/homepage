## Summary

Overnight, an electrical short in game hardware at one of our venues sent a surge through the
building and killed every UPS battery at once. Nothing shut down cleanly. Facilities had
power back by about 7 a.m. The venue's on-prem Kubernetes cluster did not come back with it.
It was an RKE cluster, managed by a central Rancher server in the cloud.

That cluster ran the message bus (RabbitMQ) that starts every game from a guest's tablet. No
bus, no games. It also ran pricing, bay access codes and the visitor service. I was the only
person working the Kubernetes layer. Services came back at about 9:45 that night.

## Impact

- Games wouldn't start for most of the business day: black tablets, and nothing on the TVs.
- Guests got about 15 hours of degraded service after power returned. About 28 hours passed
  between the moment I spotted the outage and the last cleanup step the next morning.
- Point of sale, Wi-Fi and phones were broken at the same time. Other teams and vendors owned
  those, and they muddied the picture all day.

## What made it hard

- **Three unrelated failures stacked on one power event.** They were an expired server
  certificate, a third-party chart repo that silently dropped old versions, and a second
  internal certificate nobody was tracking. Each one hid the next.
- **The error message lied.** "User cannot get clusters" reads as RBAC, so I spent hours
  chasing permission and network theories. The real problem was Rancher's own admission
  webhook failing TLS.
- **I was alone on the Kubernetes layer for about 18 hours.** The venue was half-open, other
  teams were fighting point of sale and the network, and the office VPN circuit was down too.
- **There was no infrastructure as code for venue clusters.** That work had been deferred, so
  a rebuild meant hours of manual steps.

## The turn

At one point Rancher wouldn't let an admin delete anything. Not a user, not a cluster object.
Nothing. That's when it clicked: this couldn't be permissions, because the admin account
couldn't do anything anywhere. So I stopped asking "who is allowed to do this?" and started
asking "what sits in the request path and can say no?" In Rancher, that's the validating
admission webhook. Its serving certificate was a second one-year certificate that nobody had a
reminder for.

What I wrote to the team that night:

> You know how we have alerts/reminders set up to cycle the rancher SSL certificate? … Well it turns out there's a second thing.

## Why I recovered the way I did

- **Timebox, then rebuild.** A clean venue cluster was a known quantity. The corrupted one
  wasn't, so we agreed the cutoff before going deeper.
- **Deleting the cluster object by stripping its finalizers was a one-way door.** I only did
  it after deciding to rebuild, and I wouldn't experiment on any other venue to confirm a
  theory.
- **I upgraded Rancher before opening, with a backup taken first.** That moved us onto the
  release line that issues 10-year internal certificates, during the window when no guests
  were affected.

## What I got wrong

1. The Rancher server certificate lapsed even though a reminder existed. Expiry tracking was
   a calendar habit, not monitoring, and habits lapse.
2. I had deferred the scripted rebuild path, and that cost hours here.
3. During cleanup the next morning I stacked a "while I'm in here" MetalLB upgrade onto a
   system I'd only just recovered. It caused a short second outage, entirely of my own making.

## What changed

- **The certificate problem was fixed for good.** After the upgrade I rotated every
  certificate in every cluster to 10-year validity, added a runbook step to rotate after every
  new cluster build, and set up automated expiry checks on cluster and component certificates.
  A few months later a cluster certificate turned up three days before expiry. It was renewed
  with a revised version of the same script, with no outage.
- **The postmortem with leadership two weeks later** produced a power-outage procedure for
  the venue and the support center, plus automated escalation. Chat notifications had failed
  people that week.
- **Infrastructure as code for venue clusters went from "someday" to required.** Two years
  later I was building venue clusters with Terraform.
- **I don't trust third-party Helm repos I don't mirror.** Not anymore.

## What I'd tell someone else

- When an admin can't do anything anywhere, stop debugging permissions and look at what
  sits in the request path.
- Inventory every certificate, not just the one on the front door.
- Agree on the timebox and the rebuild criteria before you start digging.
- Don't stack upgrades onto a system you just brought back.
